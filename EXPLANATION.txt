===============================================================================
PART 1 - THE BIG IDEA
===============================================================================

Two bodies pulling on each other with gravity (Earth + Sun) is a "solved"
problem: Newton showed the orbit is always an ellipse, a parabola or a
hyperbola, and there is an exact formula for it.

Add ONE more body and everything changes. In the 1880s Henri Poincare showed
there is no general formula for three bodies. The motion is usually CHAOTIC:
a change in the 15th decimal place of a starting position eventually gives a
completely different future (the "butterfly effect").

So instead of a formula we SIMULATE: we start from known positions and
velocities, and move time forward in tiny steps, calculating the forces at
every step. A computer can do millions of steps per second.

What this program does, over and over:

    1. pick random starting positions and velocities for the 3 bodies
    2. simulate them for up to 500,000 steps
    3. stop early if two bodies COLLIDE or one is EJECTED (thrown out)
    4. write the result to a CSV file, and mark long-lived systems as
       "High" or "Very High" interest

Most random systems fall apart quickly. The rare ones that last a long time
are the interesting ones - that's what we are searching for.


===============================================================================
PART 2 - THE PHYSICS AND THE EQUATIONS
===============================================================================

2.1 Vectors
-----------
Each body i has
    position      r_i = (x, y, z)
    velocity      v_i = (vx, vy, vz)      how fast r changes
    acceleration  a_i = (ax, ay, az)      how fast v changes
In the code these are 3x3 tables: one row per body, one column per x/y/z.


2.2 Newton's law of gravitation
-------------------------------
The size of the pull between two masses:

                  G * m1 * m2
            F  =  -----------
                      r^2

G = gravitational constant, r = distance between them. Double the distance
and the pull becomes 4 times weaker (inverse-square law).

Force has a DIRECTION too: body i is pulled towards body j. The vector from
i to j is  d = r_j - r_i  and its length is r = |d|. A vector of length 1
in that direction is d / r. So the force vector is

                  G * m_i * m_j       d         G * m_i * m_j * d
            F  =  -------------  *  -----   =   -----------------
                       r^2            r                r^3

That is why the code divides by r CUBED: r^2 from the law, and one more r
to turn d into a direction of length 1.


2.3 Newton's second law  (F = m a)
----------------------------------
Dividing by the mass of body i gives its acceleration - and m_i cancels out:

                  G * m_j * (r_j - r_i)
            a_i = ---------------------      (for each other body j, then add up)
                          r^3

A feather and a hammer fall at the same rate: your own mass doesn't matter.


2.4 Newton's third law  (equal and opposite)
--------------------------------------------
The pull of j on i is equal and opposite to the pull of i on j. So for each
PAIR we work out   G / r^3   once and use it for both bodies:

            a_i  +=  m_j * (G / r^3) * d
            a_j  -=  m_i * (G / r^3) * d

3 bodies have only 3 pairs (1-2, 1-3, 2-3). The original code looped over
all 6 ordered combinations and did every calculation twice.


2.5 Softening
-------------
If two bodies get very close, r -> 0 and 1/r^3 -> infinity, and the computer
produces garbage. "Softening" adds a tiny number epsilon (0.01):

                      G * m_j * d
            a_i  =  -----------------------
                    (r^2 + epsilon^2)^(3/2)

When r is large, epsilon^2 = 0.0001 makes no difference. When r = 0 the
bottom is epsilon^3, not zero, so nothing explodes.


2.6 Units
---------
We use G = 1 and masses 1.008, 4.003, 15.995 (the atomic masses of H, He and
O, used here just as numbers). These are "simulation units": only ratios
matter, so there are no metres or seconds - just "1 unit of time".


2.7 Moving forward in time - the Euler method
---------------------------------------------
Velocity is the rate of change of position, acceleration is the rate of change
of velocity. For a tiny time step dt:

            new position  =  position  +  velocity     * dt
            new velocity  =  velocity  +  acceleration * dt

This is the Euler method. Problem: it assumes the velocity and acceleration
stay constant for the whole step, but they don't (the bodies are moving
along curves). Each step makes a small error and the errors add up. A
circular orbit simulated with Euler slowly spirals outwards.


2.8 Runge-Kutta 4 (RK4) - a much better step
--------------------------------------------
RK4 "tests the slope" four times during one step and takes a weighted
average, like Simpson's rule in integration:

    k1 = slope at the START of the step
    k2 = slope at the MIDDLE, using k1 to get there
    k3 = slope at the MIDDLE again, using k2 (a better guess)
    k4 = slope at the END, using k3

    new value = old value + dt * (k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4) / 6

For our bodies, "value" is position AND velocity together, and
    slope of position = velocity
    slope of velocity = acceleration (from the gravity formula above)

Written out (this is exactly what the code does):

    k1v = a(r)                                  k1r = v
    k2v = a(r + k1r*dt/2)                       k2r = v + k1v*dt/2
    k3v = a(r + k2r*dt/2)                       k3r = v + k2v*dt/2
    k4v = a(r + k3r*dt)                         k4r = v + k3v*dt

    r_new = r + dt/6 * (k1r + 2*k2r + 2*k3r + k4r)
    v_new = v + dt/6 * (k1v + 2*k2v + 2*k3v + k4v)

Accuracy: the total error is proportional to dt^4. Halve dt and the error
becomes 2^4 = 16 times smaller. (Euler: only 2 times smaller.)

Cost: 4 gravity calculations per step.

Trick used in the new code: k1 of the NEXT step is the acceleration at the
END of this step - so we calculate it once at the end of the step and reuse
it. As a bonus that calculation measures all three pair distances, which is
exactly what the collision check needs, so collision checking is free.


2.9 Leapfrog - a cheaper step (optional)
----------------------------------------
"Kick - drift - kick":

    v_half = v + a(r) * dt/2            kick   (half a step of velocity change)
    r_new  = r + v_half * dt            drift  (full step of movement)
    v_new  = v_half + a(r_new) * dt/2   kick   (the other half)

Only ONE new gravity calculation per step (a(r_new) is reused as a(r) of
the next step), so it's about 4 times cheaper than RK4. It is less accurate
per step, but it is "symplectic": its energy error wobbles up and down
instead of slowly drifting away. Planet simulations use it for that reason.
Choose it with  integrator = "leapfrog".


2.10 Adaptive time step
-----------------------
A fixed dt = 0.001 is fine when the bodies are far apart, but in a close pass
they move so fast that one step jumps a large part of the orbit, and RK4
gets it badly wrong. It's like driving: you slow down for a sharp bend.

For every pair we estimate how long it takes for things to change a lot:

    free-fall time   t_ff = sqrt( r^3 / (G * (m_i + m_j)) )
                     (roughly the time to fall together - Kepler's 3rd law
                      says the orbit period is proportional to this)
    crossing time    t_cross = r / (relative speed)

and use

    step = adaptive_accuracy * (smallest t_ff or t_cross),  but never more than dt

With adaptive_accuracy = 0.02, one full orbit of a close pair is split into
roughly 300 small steps, however tight it is. Far apart, the step is just dt. We still COUNT progress in units of dt
(so "500,000 steps" still means 500 time units), and the rules are the same.

Measured on the first 300 cases: the same CPU time as fixed steps, but the
typical energy error fell from 0.0095 to 0.0000002.


2.11 Energy - our accuracy meter
--------------------------------
Kinetic energy (energy of motion) of each body:     KE = 1/2 * m * v^2
Gravitational potential energy of each pair:        PE = - G * m_i * m_j / r
(with softening: r is replaced by sqrt(r^2 + epsilon^2))

    Total energy  E = KE1 + KE2 + KE3 + PE12 + PE13 + PE23

PE is negative: you must ADD energy to pull two bodies apart. In real physics
E never changes (conservation of energy). In a simulation it changes a little
because of the step errors. So

                       | E_now - E_start |
    energy error  =    -------------------
                           | E_start |

tells us how much to trust a result:
    1e-7  (0.00001 %)  -> trustworthy
    1e-4  (0.01 %)     -> the program prints a warning
    1e-2  (1 %)        -> the result is probably a numerical artefact

It is checked every 50 steps and the largest value is saved in the CSV.


2.12 Centre of mass
-------------------
The "balance point" of the three bodies:

         m1*r1 + m2*r2 + m3*r3
    R =  ---------------------
             m1 + m2 + m3

With no outside forces it moves in a straight line at constant speed, so we
measure "how far away" from here, and the visualiser keeps it in the middle
of the screen.


2.13 Collision
--------------
If any pair gets closer than collision_dist (0.05) -> "Collision".
Checked every step. The original only checked every 50th step, and two
bodies can fly past each other in less than 50 steps. It missed most
collisions: half of the first 30 cases got the wrong result, often
"Ejection", because the too-close pass flung a body out.


2.14 Ejection - is the body really escaping?
--------------------------------------------
Being far away is not enough: a ball thrown upwards is far away at the top,
but it comes back. A body k escapes only if it has more than the escape
energy. We treat the other two bodies as ONE object of mass M = m_i + m_j
at their centre of mass, and use the two-body energy:

                                       G * m_k * M
    E_k = 1/2 * mu * v_rel^2    -     -----------
                                           r

    mu = m_k * M / (m_k + M)        "reduced mass"
    v_rel, r = speed and distance of body k relative to that object

E_k > 0 means its speed is above the escape velocity  sqrt(2 G (m_k + M) / r).
We also check it is moving AWAY:   r . v > 0   (dot product positive =
angle between position and velocity less than 90 degrees).

So "Ejection" = further than ejection_dist (10) AND E_k > 0 AND moving away.


2.15 Skipping hopeless systems (unbound, E >= 0)
------------------------------------------------
First we remove the energy of the whole system drifting through space
(1/2 * total mass * speed of centre of mass^2), which can never pull the
bodies apart. What's left is the "internal energy" E_int.

A theorem called the Lagrange-Jacobi identity says the "spread" of the
system, I = sum of m * (distance from centre of mass)^2, obeys

    d^2 I / dt^2  =  2 * KE_internal + 2 * E_int   >=   2 * E_int

If E_int > 0 the spread is always accelerating outwards, so the system MUST
fly apart. There is no point simulating it, so it's recorded as
"Unbound (skipped)". (With the default masses this is rare - the heavy
oxygen body holds most systems together - but it costs almost nothing.)


2.16 Interest levels
--------------------
    survived fewer than  min_steps (50,000)              -> Low
    survived at least 50,000 but then collided/ejected   -> High
    survived all long_test_steps (500,000)               -> Very High


===============================================================================
PART 3 - WALKING THROUGH three_body_search.py
===============================================================================

The file is split into sections, top to bottom:

PARAMETERS
    A list of every setting: its name, default value, what kind of value it
    must be (number > 0, true/false, list of 3 numbers ...) and a short help
    text. The menu, the checking and settings.json are all built from this
    one list, so there's only one place to add a new setting.
    PRESETS holds the famous example systems. CSV_HEADERS lists the columns.

PHYSICS ENGINE
    _accelerations()     the gravity formula of 2.3-2.5, one loop over the
                         3 pairs (Newton's 3rd law). Also returns the
                         smallest distance, for the collision check.
    _total_energy()      the energy formula of 2.11
    _escaping_body()     the escape test of 2.14
    _step_size_limit()   the time-scales of 2.10
    _simulate()          the main loop: repeat { choose step size -> RK4 or
                         leapfrog step -> collision check -> every 50 steps:
                         energy + ejection check } until something happens
                         or 500,000 steps are done.

    The @njit line above each function is NUMBA. Normal Python is slow at
    loops, because for every single "a + b" it has to look up what type a and
    b are, create a new number object, and so on. Numba translates the
    function into machine code (the language the CPU speaks) the first time
    it runs, and saves it (cache=True) for next time. The machine code is
    about 1,400 times faster than the original.
    Inside the loop nothing new is created: the work tables (acc, k2, k3 ...)
    are made once per case and reused, and the maths is written as plain
    numbers instead of creating dozens of little NumPy arrays every step.

RUNNING ONE CASE
    random_initial_conditions(case_id)
        Makes random numbers with "seed" = case_id. A seed makes a random
        generator repeatable: seed 42 always gives the same numbers. So
        case 42 can be rebuilt any time, and case N here starts exactly
        like case N in the original script.
    run_simulation()    prepares the numbers and calls the engine
    evaluate_case()     energy check (2.15), run, then build the CSV row.
                        Starting values are saved at full precision
                        (the original rounded to 8 decimals, not enough to
                        replay a chaotic system).
    interest_level()    the rules of 2.16
    rest()              the pause after each case (throttle_delay and
                        cpu_limit_percent): e.g. at 25 % it rests 3x as long
                        as it just worked.
    lower_priority()    asks Windows to run us at "below normal" priority.

SETTINGS
    load_settings()   reads settings.json and checks every value with
                      coerce(), which gives clear messages like
                      "dt must be greater than 0".
    settings_menu()   the numbered menu. startup_prompt() is the
                      10-second "press C" countdown.

FILES
    prepare_csv()        creates a CSV with its header, or checks an old one
                         has the same columns. If the program was killed in
                         the middle of writing a line, that half line is
                         removed.
    find_resume_point()  reads the LAST line of the CSV (only the end of the
                         file, so it's fast even for huge files) to find where
                         to carry on. The CSV is the "source of truth"; the
                         progress file is just a backup note.
    save_progress()      writes to a temporary file then renames it - a
                         rename is instant, so the file is never half-written.

THE SEARCH
    _run_serial()     one case after another (workers = 1)
    _run_parallel()   several worker processes, like several cooks in a
                      kitchen. Cases are handed out in batches of 20 (sending
                      each case separately wastes time on "talking"). Results
                      can finish in any order, so they wait in `finished`
                      until they can be written in case-number order - that
                      keeps the CSV tidy and resuming reliable.
    run_search()      opens the CSV files, prints the banner, and has
                      record() which writes each row, prints it and every
                      100 cases a status line.
    run_custom()      runs your one custom system.

MAIN
    Reads the command line (--setup, --set, --preset ...), loads and checks
    the settings, shows the countdown/menu, then runs the search or the
    custom case. Ctrl+C is caught so progress is always saved.


===============================================================================
PART 4 - WALKING THROUGH visualize_case.py
===============================================================================

1. find_case() searches the CSV files for the case id.
2. setup_from_row() reads the masses, starting positions/velocities AND the
   physics settings used (dt, integrator, timestep ...) from that row.
3. It runs the SAME engine twice: once quickly to find how the case ends,
   then again recording the positions every few steps (about 1,500 frames).
4. It subtracts the centre of mass from every frame, so the system stays in
   the middle of the screen.
5. build_figure() draws:
     left:        the 3 bodies (size grows with mass) with fading trails
     top right:   distance between each pair over time (log scale),
                  with the collision distance as a red dashed line.
                  A dip to the red line = collision.
     bottom right: the facts about the case
6. matplotlib's FuncAnimation calls update(k) for frame k, which moves the
   dots, trims the trails and slowly turns the 3D camera.
7. --save writes a .gif / .mp4 animation, or a .png picture of the whole path.


===============================================================================
PART 5 - READING THE RESULTS
===============================================================================

3body_all_cases.csv has one row per case, 3body_high_interest.csv only the
High / Very High ones. Columns:

    case_id              case number (also its random seed)
    m1, m2, m3           masses
    p1_x ... p3_z        starting positions
    v1_x ... v3_z        starting velocities
    survival_steps       how many steps (of size dt) it lasted
    end_reason           Survived / Collision / Ejection / Numerical error /
                         Unbound (skipped)
    interest_level       Low / High / Very High
    survival_time        survival_steps * dt
    event_detail         which bodies collided / which was ejected
    min_distance         closest any two bodies ever got
    energy_error         largest energy error (see 2.11) - check this before
                         trusting a result!
    initial_energy       total energy at the start
    G, dt, softening, integrator, timestep    the settings used

Open them in Excel, or watch any case with
    python visualize_case.py <case_id>
